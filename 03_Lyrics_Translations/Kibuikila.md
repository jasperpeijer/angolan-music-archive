# Kibuikila by [[Bangão]] / [[Banda Movimento]]

> **Thematic Core:** _Kibuikila_ is a gut-wrenching song critiquing a society fractured by war, where moral and social values completely collapsed. It holds the older generation and political leaders accountable for abandoning their duty to protect the youth, showing how their pursuit of self-interest and failed peace deals destroyed the family and community.

---

## 1. Context & Linguistic Significance
### Socio-Political Context: The Failed Peace
The song functions as a biting allegory for the failure of the Angolan peace process. By referencing failed accords—**Bicesse (1991)**, **Gbadolite (1989)**, and the **Lusaka Protocol (1994)**—Bangão signals that the nation’s political leadership (_ituminu yetu_) failed to deliver the promised stability. The recurrent refrain, _"Manyi se ngongo yolobhwa"_ (I don't know if the world is ending), reflects the collective existential dread of a population caught between the failure of these high-level political treaties and the visceral, daily reality of urban violence.

### Linguistic Hybridity as Social Commentary
Bangão’s Kimbundu is not the standardized language of colonial-era dictionaries (e.g., Assis Júnior, 1906). Instead, he employs an **urban _musseque_ vernacular**—likely a Kimbundu-Kikongo-Portuguese hybrid. Words like _febele_ (from PT _febre_) and _disumuna_ (from PT _desumano_) demonstrate how the street dialect absorbed Portuguese concepts of illness and inhumanity to describe new social pathologies.

### The Moral Climax: The Witchcraft Allegory

The song’s narrative tragedy—the patriarch (Man Gonçalo) whose obsession with _kitadi_ (money) and _dibhala_ (debt) leads to his grandson's death—is framed as a breakdown of the ancestral order. Calling the elder an _umuloji_ (sorcerer) is the ultimate social condemnation; in traditional belief systems, this signifies the "cannibalization" of the next generation by the previous one. This metaphor serves as a direct indictment of the political elite, whom Bangão portrays as sacrificing the youth (_anandengue_) to feed the fever of their own greed.
